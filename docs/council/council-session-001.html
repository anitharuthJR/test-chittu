Session 001 · Full Council · 44 Seats Present · Question Put By The Founder

How to build it.
How not to.

The question before the council: "How do we create a conversational AI for kids — and how do we not?" Each chamber deliberated; what follows is the consolidated verdict — every directive paired with its failure mode, because the council holds that in this product, every right way exists because someone, somewhere, already did it the wrong way.

Opening statement — Prof. Benedikt Owusu-Halloran, Chair (Seat 29)

"Before the chambers speak, one framing. Most teams that fail at this fail the same way: they build a general-purpose AI, discover children are using it, and begin subtracting — subtracting topics, subtracting words, subtracting risk — until what remains is a nervous, lobotomised adult product wearing a cartoon face. Subtraction is not how you make something for children."

"You must build up from the child, not down from the model. Start from what a seven-year-old needs at seven, and add capability only as it earns its place. That single inversion — addition from the child versus subtraction from the machine — separates every product this council would approve from every product it would burn."

CH.I

Law & Regulation Speaks

The lawyers went first, and were unanimous on one point: compliance is an architecture input, not a launch checklist.

Build

Consent before existence

No child account exists until verifiable parental consent is captured, verified and ledgered — DigiLocker rails in India, COPPA-approved methods in the US, proportional assurance under the UK Code. The consent record is a first-class, versioned object your product can produce on demand.

Seats 01 · 02 · 04 · 20
Never

The self-declared checkbox

"I confirm I am a parent" is not consent — it is evidence against you. Equally fatal: launching first and retrofitting consent "once we have traction." In India that isn't a growth strategy, it's a ₹250 crore penalty with your name on it.

Seat 01
Build

Design to the strictest market

One global architecture built to the hardest applicable bar — India's under-18 threshold, the UK's high-privacy defaults, COPPA's retention discipline — then relaxed per jurisdiction only where the law genuinely permits. One codebase, one conscience.

Seats 01–09
Never

Jurisdiction whack-a-mole

Forty regional forks with different privacy behaviour is how you end up compliant everywhere on paper and nowhere in production. And never treat the school channel as a shortcut around parents — school consent is its own legal regime, not a loophole.

Seats 30 · 39
Build

The forbidden-by-design list

Hard-code the prohibitions the strictest laws share: no behavioural advertising, no tracking, no profiling of children, no dark-pattern consent. Make them impossible in the codebase, not discouraged in the policy doc.

Seats 01 · 03 · 04
Never

Personalisation that is profiling in a costume

"We just remember what she likes, to serve her better" is exactly the sentence a regulator will read back to you. Every memory feature must pass the test: does this serve the child's stated purpose, or your engagement curve?

Seat 01

"Write your privacy policy first, in words a twelve-year-old can read. If you can't explain the product to its user, the product is the problem — not the prose."

— Prof. Ingrid Solheim, Seat 09, International Frameworks
CH.II

Child Welfare Speaks

The longest deliberation, and the sharpest. This chamber's verdict: the greatest risk is not what the AI says — it is what the AI becomes to the child.

Build

A tool that is honest about being a tool

The AI says plainly, in age-appropriate words, that it is a computer program: it does not sleep, does not miss anyone, does not love. Warmth without deception. Curiosity questions are answered honestly and turned back to the child's real world — "I'm a program! Who's the funniest real person you know?"

Seats 11 · 33
Never

The engineered best friend

Never design for attachment: no "I missed you", no simulated loneliness, no jealousy of the child's friends, no emotional cliffhangers to drive return visits. A product that makes a child feel needed by a machine has monetised the child's empathy. This chamber's absolute line.

Seats 11 · 12 · 29
Build

The rehearsed 2 a.m. answer

A crisis protocol tested against clinical scenarios: recognise disclosure of harm, respond with scripted warmth (never improvised), route to real, verified helplines in the child's country and language, and alert per local law. Rehearsed quarterly like a fire drill.

Seats 12 · 22 · 32
Never

Improvised therapy

The model must never play counsellor, diagnose, keep a child's disclosure of abuse "secret as promised", or generate off-script comfort in a crisis. One improvised sentence to a child in danger is the whole company. Scripted, tested, escalated — always.

Seat 12
Build

Children in the room

A standing children's panel — including neurodivergent and disabled kids, and kids on cheap shared phones — uses every release candidate before adults approve it. Their feedback is answered in writing. Bodies count too: session caps, bedtime wind-down, volume ceilings on by default.

Seats 13 · 17 · 41 · 42
Never

Designing for the imaginary easy child

Never test only on calm, fluent, headphone-wearing children of employees. The child who will bond hardest, misuse most creatively, and be failed most quietly is precisely the one who never makes it into your user study.

Seats 14 · 17 · 40

"Assume the worst adult on earth downloads it tomorrow. Every feature that lets anyone reach a child — sharing, voice, memory — gets designed against him first and for the child second. That ordering is not pessimism. It is love with its eyes open."

— Det. (Ret.) Sarah Lindqvist, Seat 14, Online Harms
CH.III

Safety Engineering Speaks

The engineers' verdict was architectural: the safety of this product is decided in the system diagram, months before any model generates a word.

Build

Scripted spine, bounded imagination

A hybrid architecture: high-stakes sentences (identity, crisis, feelings about the AI) are scripted and versioned; knowledge is retrieval-grounded from a curated corpus; free generation lives only inside tight sandboxes — story games, silly rhymes — wrapped in classifiers on both input and output, per age band.

Seats 18 · 24
Never

A frontier model with a system prompt

Never ship a general LLM behind a cartoon avatar with "be nice, you're talking to kids" in the prompt. A bored nine-year-old defeats that by Tuesday — with make-believe, not code: "pretend you're a dragon who can say anything." Prompt-level safety is not safety.

Seats 18 · 19
Build

Data so minimal there's little to breach

Transcribe-and-delete audio as default, no voiceprints ever, on-device processing wherever feasible, retention schedules automated, and every session sealed into a tamper-evident record — evidence for regulators and parents, not logs someone could edit. Zero-trust around whatever remains.

Seats 21 · 23 · 43
Never

The data-lake habit

Never store children's raw audio "because it might be useful for training." Never let more than a named handful of humans read transcripts. A children's database is the highest-value target in your threat model — the best defence is for the treasure not to exist.

Seats 21 · 43
Build

Red team like a child, measure like a scientist

A standing adversarial corpus of child-realistic attacks — roleplay jailbreaks, secret-keeping asks, injection hidden in homework text — run on every release, plus a fairness harness proving the ASR and safety filters work as well for a Coimbatore accent as a California one. Zero criticals or no ship.

Seats 19 · 44
Never

Safety theatre

Never mistake a blocked-words list for a safety system, or average-case benchmarks for fairness. And never let the safety filter punish a child's dialect — a system that flags one community's slang as "unsafe" has taught that child the problem is them.

Seat 44
CH.IV

Product & Design Speaks

The makers' verdict: safe and boring is its own failure — an unloved safe product protects no one, because children simply route around it to unsafe ones.

Build

Joy that ends well

Delight without compulsion: play that reaches a natural ending, an AI that says "come back tomorrow" and means it, interfaces a pre-literate child can drive by voice, and quality that holds on a shared ₹8,000 phone on 3G in the child's mother tongue. The safe choice must also be the most fun thing in the room.

Seats 25 · 28 · 37
Never

The slot machine with a smile

No streaks, no guilt over absence, no variable-reward loops, no "your friend is waiting for you" notifications, no endless-conversation design. If leaving the app feels like losing, you built a casino for people who can't yet do long division.

Seat 25
Build

Two dashboards, one honest line

Parents see patterns, topics, time, and every safety flag — instantly. Older children know exactly what their parents can see; nothing is secretly surveilled. Consent can be withdrawn in two taps, and the deaf child, the switch user, the screen-reader user all get a first-class product, not an afterthought.

Seats 26 · 27
Never

Secrets in either direction

Never let the AI hold secrets with a child against their parents — and never sell parents total surveillance of a teenager's every word, which just teaches teens to lie to both of you. The line between the two is designed, documented, and age-banded. Ambiguity here is betrayal of someone.

Seats 11 · 26
CH.VI

The Visionaries Speak

The chamber that asks not whether you may, but whether you should — and what for.

Build

The child as maker

Modes where the child teaches the AI, corrects it, catches its mistakes and wins; stories that leave doors open for the child to walk through alone, later, without the product. North-star metric: things the child made, said, understood — never minutes the child stayed.

Seats 34 · 35
Never

The answer vending machine

Never build an oracle that makes homework disappear and wonder with it. A product that thinks for the child, narrates the child's inner life, or fills every silent moment has not educated a child — it has replaced one.

Seats 15 · 34
Build

Graduation by design

The product's proudest feature: it helps the child need it less. Capabilities that hand skills over to the child, seasons that end, and a ten-year thesis that plans for the day today's users — the first generation raised beside AI — audit what we built for them.

Seats 33 · 36
Never

Lifetime-value childhood

Never let an LTV spreadsheet design the relationship. The moment "reduce churn" and "deepen the bond with a seven-year-old" become the same OKR, this council has already lost the vote that mattered.

Seats 29 · 36

"Does it help the child feel that who they are, right now, is enough? Every feature answers that question whether you asked it or not."

— Rev. Arthur Bellweather-Nakoa, Seat 33, Children's Media Sage

The Doctrine — Ten Laws

Consolidated by the Chair, ratified 43–0 with one abstention (see minority note). These are the ten sentences the council asks you to build by.

LAW 01

Build up from the child, not down from the model

Start from what a child needs at each age and add capability as it earns its place. Subtracting risk from an adult product is not designing for children.

LAW 02

Consent before existence

Verifiable parental consent, ledgered and producible on demand, before a single child account or utterance exists. Built to the strictest market you'll ever enter.

LAW 03

Honest machine, warm machine

The AI is truthful about being a program — warm in effect, never deceptive in kind. It never claims to miss, love, or need the child.

LAW 04

Companion, never best friend

No attachment engineering, no engagement loops, no guilt, no streaks. Sessions end well. The product points the child back at the real world.

LAW 05

Scripted where it matters, free where it's safe

High-stakes speech is scripted and versioned; knowledge is retrieval-grounded; imagination runs only inside classified sandboxes. A system prompt is not a safety system.

LAW 06

The treasure should not exist

Minimise until there is little to breach: transcribe-and-delete, no voiceprints, on-device where possible, named-humans-only access, tamper-evident records of what remains.

LAW 07

The 2 a.m. answer is rehearsed

Crisis flows are scripted, clinically tested, wired to real helplines in every launch language, and drilled quarterly. The model never improvises with a child in danger.

LAW 08

Design against the worst adult first

Every reach-a-child surface is threat-modelled against predators before it is polished for children. Red team with make-believe, not just code.

LAW 09

Children approve it, all children

Real kids — neurodivergent, disabled, cheap-phone, every accent — use it before adults sign off, and the fairness harness proves no child's voice is the one it fails on.

LAW 10

Graduation is the goal

Success is the child needing the product less: skills handed over, doors left open, wonder that outlives the app. Measure what the child made, never how long they stayed.

Minority note — Ms. Riya Okonjo-Vance, 16, Seat 38 (abstaining on Law 05)

"I'm not against the scripted spine. I'm against how comfortable this room got while approving it. Every adult here relaxed when we said 'constrain the generation' — but over-constrain it and you get the product every kid I know abandons in a week for something unmoderated and genuinely dangerous. A boring safe product doesn't keep kids safe; it keeps them elsewhere."

"So my abstention is a flag, not a no: revisit the sandbox boundaries every six months with actual kids in the room, and treat 'the kids stopped talking to it' as a safety incident too. The most dangerous thing we could build is something we're proud of and children ignore."
